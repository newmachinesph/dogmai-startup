The payments problem nobody will solve

Infrastructure·Jun 2026·6 min·The founder's desk

Ask a hundred practitioners what nearly ended their business and you will not hear about competition, or AI, or even the algorithm. You will hear a processor's name, and a date, and how much money was frozen when the email arrived.

Contraband by keyword

Stripe's restricted-business list names “psychic services and fortune tellers” — prior written approval required, banned outright in some jurisdictions. Square and PayPal hold similar postures. Practitioners have organized petitions over accounts cancelled for the words psychic, fortune teller, or occult appearing on a checkout page. Not fraud signals. Vocabulary.

The distribution story rhymes. Apple's Guideline 4.3 files fortune-telling with spam; the astrology app Struck reported roughly ten rejections before making it through. And Meta's Personal Attributes policy quietly kills the ad creative this trade would naturally write — you can't imply you know a viewer's religion or emotional state, which is roughly what “Your Saturn return is coming” does.

So the picture: a $2.3 billion US industry — 103,340 businesses, growing 5.5% a year — treated as contraband by the payment layer, spam by the distribution layer, and a policy violation by the marketing layer. Everyone in it is one automated keyword scan from a frozen quarter.

A market's worst infrastructure gap is its best company's founding document.

Why it stays unsolved

Because for everyone positioned to solve it, it's a bad trade. A mainstream processor gains nothing by re-underwriting a category its risk models flagged — the upside is basis points, the downside is a chargeback scandal in a category regulators already watch. A horizontal creator platform (Patreon, Circle, Kajabi) could add spiritual templates in a quarter, but negotiating high-risk underwriting for fortune-telling merchants means staking their brand on the exact category their counsel would advise against. And a solo practitioner has no leverage at all — they are, to an underwriter, a rounding error with a tarot deck.

The only actor with the right incentives is one whose entire business is this category: someone who aggregates hundreds of practices into an underwritable book, codes them honestly, runs conservative refund and chargeback controls, and — this is the part we find genuinely elegant — submits its compliance layer as underwriting evidence. Guardrail logs, disclosure records, attestation manifests: the same apparatus that satisfies a regulator reads, to an underwriter, as a risk profile no individual merchant could ever present.

Risk and moat are the same fact

Our market map calls payments the highest-probability killer of this company, and we've published that assessment — along with the mitigation sequence (merchant-of-record and high-risk relationships secured before scaling) and the tripwire: if the rails can't be secured by 2028, we pivot to software-only atop practitioners' existing approved processors. A narrower company, but a live one.

But if the rails hold — and the underwriting logic above is why we believe they will — then payments becomes the moat that horizontal platforms cannot cross without becoming us. Features get copied in a quarter. Underwriting relationships in a restricted category, backed by a compliance apparatus and two years of clean chargeback data, do not.

That's why Commerce ships first, before the glamorous modules. A practice that can't get paid doesn't need a voice model. It needs a bank that will take its money — and a vendor willing to be the one who asked.

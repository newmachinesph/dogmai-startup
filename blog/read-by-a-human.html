“Read by a human” is becoming law

Regulation·Jul 2026·7 min·The founder's desk

There's a category of startup luck that never gets named in pitch decks: the regulation you would have lobbied for arrives on its own, three years early, while your competitors are still building the thing it prohibits.

That is what has happened to the human/AI boundary in intimate services — and almost nobody in the spiritual-services world has noticed yet.

Three laws, one demand

Start in California. SB 243, signed in October 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, says that where a reasonable person would be misled into believing they're talking with a human, the operator must give a clear and conspicuous notification that the companion chatbot is artificially generated. It mandates self-harm protocols. And — the part that should focus every operator's mind — it carries a private right of action. Not an agency that might get around to you. Plaintiffs' lawyers, on contingency.

New York got there first: General Business Law §1700, the AI Companion Models statute, took effect November 5, 2025, with its own disclosure and safety duties. Utah and Maine moved the same year. Our market map's projection is ten-plus states and a likely FTC rule by around 2030 — and nothing in the legislative calendar since has argued otherwise.

Across the Atlantic, Article 5 of the EU AI Act has been in force since February 2025, prohibiting AI that manipulates or exploits vulnerability into significant psychological or financial harm — with fines up to €35M or 7% of global turnover. Commission guidance name-checks products promising unrealistic mental-health benefits and therapeutic chatbots that steer vulnerable users into expensive purchases. If you sell AI-generated predictions to emotionally vulnerable people, you are not adjacent to this text. You are its subject.

The law is converging on the exact distinction this industry has been arguing about since 2023: is there a human in this reading, or not?

Who should be worried

Consumer AI-reading apps, mostly. Their unit economics depend on the machine doing the interpreting at zero marginal cost, with just enough ambiguity about the human touch to keep the experience warm. That ambiguity is precisely what SB 243 prices as liability. Every disclosure banner they're now required to show — this is AI, not a person — is a small advertisement for the practitioners they were undercutting.

The FTC's posture compounds it. Operation AI Comply has been running since September 2024 — DoNotPay's “robot lawyer” settled, Workado's accuracy claims prosecuted — under the simplest of doctrines: deception law applies to AI, no differently. And the FTC has prosecuted fraudulent psychic operations for decades. The combination — AI claims plus psychic services — is an enforcement Venn diagram you do not want to occupy without paperwork.

Who should be delighted

Every practitioner who actually does the work — if they can prove it.

That conditional is the entire business. “A human read this” has always been the honest practitioner's differentiation; what's new is that it's becoming a legal category with a compliance apparatus around it. When disclosure-of-AI is mandatory, verified-absence-of-AI (or verified human review over disclosed AI assistance) becomes the premium tier — and the premium needs rails: identity-verified practitioners, signed manifests, tamper-evidence, independent verification. That's Attest, and it's why we built it on C2PA-style provenance rails rather than on a trust-us badge. The C2PA consortium — Adobe, BBC, Microsoft, Sony — built exactly this infrastructure for media provenance, but left no free certificate path for individuals. In our vertical, we are that path.

The market has already priced the human premium once, incidentally. CHANI runs an explicit zero-AI, all-human-written policy — no ads, no venture money — at an estimated $14M a year. That's not a values statement outperforming a business model. That's a values statement as a business model.

The window

Here's the strategic clock as we read it. Between now and roughly 2030, disclosure law spreads state by state, each new statute raising the floor and burying whoever built on the old one. The practitioners who adopt provable-human infrastructure early get years of compounding differentiation before it becomes table stakes. The ones who wait get to buy it as a commodity later — from whoever spent those years becoming the default.

We intend to be the default. That's not subtle, and it isn't meant to be.

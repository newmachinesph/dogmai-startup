The seven-year clock

Strategy·Apr 2026·9 min·The founder's desk

Every company runs on a clock, usually borrowed from a funding cycle. Ours is borrowed from something stranger: a two-thousandth anniversary.

What happens in 2033

The Vatican has confirmed it: an Extraordinary Jubilee — a Holy Year of Redemption — in 2033, marking two millennia since the Resurrection. Pope Leo XIV announced it from Istanbul in November 2025. Around it, a coordinated global evangelization push is already organizing (Global 2033, JC2033), with Pentecost Sunday, June 5, 2033, framed as “the two thousandth birthday of Christianity.”

Whatever one's beliefs, the cultural mechanics are predictable: for roughly a year, a meaningful share of global media attention will turn to existential questions. Meaning, mortality, redemption, what a life is for. The last time the calendar did anything comparable — the 2000 Great Jubilee and the millennium — the surrounding years saw a documented boom not just in Christian observance but across the whole spiritual landscape, New Age currents very much included.

The mechanism, and this is the part worth being precise about, is attention, not endorsement. Major religious anniversaries raise the cultural salience of existential questions. Salience spills over. People who would never attend a Mass still find themselves, in a year like that, wondering what their life means — and some of them book a reading.

The wave lifts everyone who is standing in the water. It drowns whoever tries to claim it.

The rule: ride, never co-opt

Here is what makes 2033 a double-edged date for this trade: the anniversary is explicitly Christian, and Catholic doctrine explicitly condemns divination — all forms, by catechism. A tarot platform running Jubilee-themed marketing would manage to be simultaneously offensive to the faithful, embarrassing to practitioners, and radioactive to the platform-policy teams at Meta and Apple who already treat this category with tongs.

So the rule, written into our roadmap as a constraint rather than a suggestion: ride the attention wave; never touch the anniversary. In practice that means Phase IV's positioning is practitioner-empowerment and wellness — the infrastructure story, the verified-human story, the “technology in service of ancient craft” story — timed to a year when editors are hungry for exactly those stories. It does not mean Jubilee imagery, Resurrection references, or any marketing that borrows a sacredness that isn't ours to borrow.

We flag this honestly in the market map: the spillover thesis is inferential. There's suggestive historical coincidence — the 2000 New Age boom alongside the Great Jubilee — but no rigorous study isolating Christian-observance spillover into non-Christian spiritual markets. We treat 2033 as a plausible tailwind and a firm deadline, never as a demand forecast. The plan works if the wave never comes. It just works louder if it does.

Why a deadline changes a company

The practical value of 2033 has less to do with the Jubilee than with what a fixed horizon does to decision-making. Seven years is long enough to build patiently — to hand-recruit twenty-five practitioners in year one, to spend two years on payments underwriting that nobody will ever see — and short enough that every quarter has to argue for its place on the clock.

It's why the roadmap runs on falsifiable gates rather than vibes: ten paying practitioners and $2–5K MRR by end of 2026, or the validation failed. $50–100K MRR with churn under 3% by 2028, or the entrenchment failed. A deliberate fund-or-stay-lean decision at $100K MRR, not a drift into whichever happens. Published tripwires for the scenarios where we're wrong — about payments, about horizontal competitors, about demand depth.

Category leadership by 2033 is, per our own research, a qualified yes: achievable if “the category” is Western practitioner infrastructure, if payments are solved early, if capital or profitability arrives by 2028, and if the B2B2C discipline holds absolutely. Four ifs, each with a date. That's not a vision statement. That's a clock — and it's already running.

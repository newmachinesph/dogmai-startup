Flat is fine: the honest bull case

Market·May 2026·8 min·The founder's desk

Every pitch deck in this category opens the same way: a hockey stick labeled “the astrology market,” usually citing a projection that lands somewhere between $25 and $30 billion by 2033. We know, because we've read them. And then we commissioned our own market research and instructed it to try to kill our company.

Here is what it found, and why we put it on our own website.

The numbers that cut against us

Belief in astrology among US adults: 27% in 2024, versus 29% in 2017. Pew's lead researcher describes it as “almost no change over the past eight years.” Gallup found 23–28% across the 1990s and 2000s — this line has been flat since before some of our design partners were born.

The religiously unaffiliated — the “nones” whose rise powers every spiritual-startup narrative — have plateaued near 29% after decades of growth. And engagement is wide but shallow: 30% of US adults consult astrology, tarot, or a fortune teller at least yearly, but only 6% weekly, and just 1% “rely a lot” on these practices for major decisions. Most people, in Pew's phrasing, do it “just for fun.”

Meanwhile the published market sizes conflict by two orders of magnitude — $153M if you count horoscope apps, $12.8–14.3B if you accept the broadest vendor definitions. We anchor on the one methodologically consistent primary series: IBISWorld's US psychic-services industry, at $2.3 billion, growing 5.5% annually across 103,340 businesses.

A market that isn't exploding also isn't a bubble. Nobody's flat line collapses.

Why this is the bull case

First: stability de-risks the demand side entirely. Thirty percent of American adults, every year, decade after decade — through recessions, through platform shifts, through the entire internet era. That is not a trend to time. It's a load-bearing feature of the culture, and you can build infrastructure on load-bearing features.

Second: the shape of the demand is precisely wrong for consumer apps and precisely right for practitioner tooling. Wide-and-shallow engagement means consumer apps fight a brutal war for the 6% who engage weekly — which is why their app-store reviews are a graveyard of paywall complaints. But the 103,340 businesses serving the whole 30%? They're underserved to the point of neglect: payment processors ban them by keyword, no vertical software exists for them, and the market is so fragmented that no firm holds even 5% share. Fragmentation plus neglect is the classic vertical-SaaS setup — it's what therapists looked like before SimplePractice rode it to 225,000 clinicians.

Third: where the demand is moving matters more than the average. 43% of women under 50 affirm astrology. Among LGBT adults, 54% consult horoscopes yearly and a third use tarot — triple the national rate. The practitioners serving these demographics skew brand-forward, digitally native, and Instagram-fluent: exactly the profile our Phase I cohort targets, and exactly the readers whose clients will check an attestation seal.

The discipline of the honest number

There's a practical reason we publish the flat line rather than the hockey stick, beyond hygiene. Companies that believe their own inflated TAM make predictable mistakes: they overbuild for demand that isn't coming, underprice to chase volume that doesn't exist, and treat the supply side — the practitioners — as interchangeable inventory. Every one of those mistakes is fatal in a trust-based trade.

A company that believes the flat line builds differently: for productivity per practitioner rather than user growth, for retention rather than acquisition, for take rates on real commerce rather than engagement metrics on borrowed attention. If demand surprises us upward — the 2033 attention wave is a genuine possibility — we'll take the upside gladly. We just refuse to need it.

Flat is fine. Flat is fundable, if you build the right company on it. And flat, printed honestly on your own website, is the cheapest trust you'll ever buy.
